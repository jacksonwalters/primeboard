How to use
  1. Start with the settings at the top. The defaults are ready to play. Choose a view, then tune sound, Sustain, and the sliders as you explore. Cantor algebra combines prefix cylinders by union or intersection; Number mixes colors; Flat plate shows patterns on a square plate; Rounded cube and Sphere show scalar surface patterns or the normal component of a metal shell’s vibration modes.
  2. Play the keyboard. Type the labeled keys, or press them with a mouse or touch. Combine keys to add colors or mode shapes, or to unite or intersect cylinders in Cantor algebra. In the mode views, holding keeps a resonant driver on: vibration builds toward a steady level, then rings down after release. The × number on a key shows its current normalized vibration strength; a fresh held key settles near ×1.0. Number uses whole-number exponents instead. Cantor cylinders depend only on which keys are active; holding or repeating a prefix does not change the set.
  3. Connect a MIDI keyboard. Plug in a USB MIDI keyboard, choose Connect keyboard, and allow MIDI access in your browser. Select the device if needed, then press its leftmost key to set the starting point. Counting black and white keys together, the next 32 keys select Cantor’s 31 prefixes and then the empty set; other views use the first 26. This browser remembers each keyboard’s starting key. Use Set first key again if you change the keyboard’s octave or transpose setting. A browser with Web MIDI support is required, such as Chrome or Chromium, over HTTPS or localhost.
  4. Play through MIDI. The on-screen note labels describe the actual sound; the physical piano keys select positions without retuning the instrument. Velocity and pitch bend do not change the mapping. A sustain pedal keeps released keys active, alongside the Sustain button. Clear resets both. Unplugging the keyboard releases its notes. MIDI performances can be recorded just like typing or touching keys; stop song playback and keep this window focused to play.
  5. Use the volume knob. A connected keyboard’s volume knob (MIDI CC7) adjusts Sound volume across its full slider range, including during songs and fullscreen. This is the default knob assignment on the M-Audio Keystation Mini 32 MK3. Keep this window focused; the slider and percentage follow the knob. Turning it up does not unmute Sound off. Volume changes are not saved in recordings; video export uses the volume selected when export starts.
  6. Keep a pattern. Turn on Sustain before releasing your keys. It keeps their drivers, tones, and patterns active. Replaying a sustained mode key adds one unit of driver weight, allowing stronger modes in a mixture. Turn Sustain off to release them. Space toggles Sustain when you are not using another control.
  7. Find musical notes. Each key shows the nearest audible note and its octave, using A4 = 440 Hz. An ≈ means the pitch is more than 5 cents from that note; 100 cents is one semitone. Hover over a key or focus it with Tab to see its frequency and signed tuning difference below the keyboard. Note names use sharps, so C♯ also names D♭. Sphere octave adjustments update the audible note labels; the natural frequency remains in the key details.
  8. Follow a scale. Choose a Scale guide and root above the keyboard. Outlined keys with underlined notes are within 25 cents of a note in that scale, across all octaves. The count shows matching keys, including repeated pitches. Some scale notes or registers may be missing. The guide only highlights: every key remains playable at its original pitch, and songs and recordings keep their tuning. In Cantor, sound follows the simplified set. A union may merge keys into a parent cylinder with its own pitch; the guide describes each key played alone.
  9. Explore Cantor algebra. Choose Union ∪ or Intersection ∩ on the second settings row. Union is the default: S + O keeps both C(100) and C(001), while S + D merges the siblings C(100) and C(101) into C(10). In Intersection, Y + D gives C(10) ∩ C(101) = C(101), and S + O gives ∅. Switch operations while holding keys or during playback to compare the results. Sustain lets you combine keys one at a time.
  10. Find a cylinder. The 26 letters follow the binary tree in order: ∅, 0, 1, 00, 01, 10, 11, and so on. The punctuation keys [ ] ; , . complete depth 4 with 1011, 1100, 1101, 1110, 1111, respectively. Q is labeled ∅ for the empty prefix, whose cylinder is the whole Cantor set. D selects 101 and all its infinite continuations. The final / key is labeled ∅ with “empty set” beneath it: it makes an Intersection empty and leaves a Union unchanged.
  11. Read the Cantor picture. Rows n=0 through n=4 show repeated middle-third removal. The diagram shows the simplified result: shadows mark the containing intervals and bright fragments mark the cylinders. An empty result has no shadow or highlighted fragments and is silent; no active keys leave the display idle and silent. The finer fragments on [0,1] are an approximation: every branch continues forever. All 31 prefixes through length 4 are playable. Sound uses one tone per cylinder in the simplified result. Deeper prefixes have higher pitches; hover or focus a key to see its frequency.
  12. Clear your keys. Clear or Escape resets your manual mixture and its sound. In fullscreen, Escape first returns to the regular view. If a song is playing, use its Stop button instead.
  13. Play in fullscreen. Use the expand button in the visualization’s upper-right corner. Your computer keyboard, MIDI controller, songs, and recordings keep playing. Show keyboard brings back the touch keys. Use the exit button or Escape to return without clearing your notes. On browsers without native fullscreen, the visualization fills the browser window.
  14. Explore the display. Drag the cube or sphere to rotate it; when it has keyboard focus, use arrow keys or Home. Pause motion freezes only the display. The second selector row chooses the surface or plate model and the plate display. Free plate uses a freely suspended aluminum sheet; Simply supported keeps the ideal supported-edge patterns. Scalar surface shows simpler mathematical patterns; Elastic shell uses the metal model. In Flat plate, Colored nodes overlays the individual patterns, Mode mixture adds their signed shapes, and Vibration strength shows the square of that mixture. The first three displays show readable strengths and fades, not fast physical vibration.
  15. Adjust sound. Sound starts on your first interaction. Use Sound on to mute, Enable sound to unmute, and Sound volume to adjust loudness. Elastic shell pitches are calculated resonances; Scalar surface uses illustrative pitches. In Elastic shell, the key’s λ label is the squared angular frequency, not its frequency in Hz. For the elastic sphere, Sound pitch can lower the audio by two or three octaves; note labels follow the audible pitch, and key details show both audible and natural frequencies.
  16. Record a performance. Press Record, play the keys, then Stop recording. Your latest take is saved in this browser; Export video saves the current visualization with sound (MP4 or WebM, depending on your browser). Export renders silently with a progress bar, without replaying the take. Speed depends on your device and visualization. You can cancel without losing the recording. Keep the page open until the download is ready. Save replay file keeps the key timings for a future replay. Replay it through any view. Recording stops after ten minutes or when you leave the page. Before replacing a take, you can export it or save its replay file.
  17. Play a song. Choose one of ten classical arrangements or Branches Return, an original Cantor piece by Codex, then Play. Selecting Branches Return opens Cantor in Union mode; you can still switch operations or views to explore. Pause, Resume, Restart, Stop, and Tempo control playback. Switching views keeps your place but changes the sound model, so pitches differ from the intended setting. Stop the song to return to manual playing.
  18. Form a sand pattern. Choose Flat plate → Sand particles, then hold a key or turn on Sustain. Six thousand grains gradually gather where vibration is weak; give the pattern several seconds to emerge. Each held key continuously drives its mode at its natural pitch. Changing keys rearranges the same grains, and chords can leave patches instead of a complete nodal curve. Release all keys to let the grains come to rest. Redistribute sand spreads them again; Clear stops the excitation while preserving their positions. Freeze motion pauses only the picture.
  19. Adjust the preview. Screen exposure changes Number and the three field-based plate displays; Hold step controls Number’s integer buildup. If your device requests reduced motion, use Resume motion or Animate to enable the visual fade.

01 / Numbers become spectra

Each key has a distinct prime pᵢ and a fixed wavelength λᵢ. A press starts at exponent 1; holding adds 1 at the selected interval, up to 32. Each exponent is an equal-radiant-power weight, not a photon count.

n = ∏ pᵢeᵢ
Sλ(λ) = ∑ eᵢ δ(λ − λᵢ)

Unique factorization recovers every spectral weight. Multiplying numbers adds their spectra, within the instrument’s range. The number 1 means no light. Sustain keeps released weights; pressing again adds to them.

02 / Spectra become perception

Light frequencies are logarithmically spaced from 700 nm red to 400 nm violet, using f = c/λ. The official CIE 1931 2° observer functions convert the sum of spectral lines to XYZ:

(X, Y, Z) = ∑ eᵢ (x̄(λᵢ), ȳ(λᵢ), z̄(λᵢ))

The 1 nm data is linearly interpolated. Mixing adds power at existing frequencies; it does not average frequencies or produce a new spectral line. Different spectra can look identical: a color alone cannot recover the number.

03 / Perception meets a screen

XYZ is transformed to linear sRGB. Most pure spectral colors are outside a screen’s gamut. We clip negative channels, apply one shared exposure-compression scale, then apply the sRGB transfer function. The preview is an approximation; the XYZ values above remain unmodified.

Doubling every exponent doubles XYZ and preserves chromaticity. Equal power does not mean equal perceived brightness, so violet and deep red can look dim. Keycap colors are normalized as a guide.

In Number, sound uses the same exponents as power weights, with one equal-tempered sine tone per prime, C3 to C♯5. Amplitude grows as √eᵢ, because power is proportional to amplitude squared. Tones add before output compression and limiting; these protect loud chords and do not change the encoded spectrum.

s(t) = A₀ ∑ √eᵢ sin(2πfᵢt + φᵢ)

Cantor algebra / Prefixes become sets

A binary prefix s selects the cylinder C(s): all infinite binary strings beginning with s. Read 0 as the left third and 1 as the right third, or replace each bit bᵢ by the ternary digit 2bᵢ. Each row removes the middle third of every remaining interval. The Cantor set C is the intersection of all these stages; the finite bars and finer fragments on [0,1] are approximations.

x = ∑ 2bᵢ / 3ⁱ   (i ≥ 1)
C(10) ∩ C(101) = C(101)
C(100) ∩ C(001) = ∅

Two cylinders intersect only when one prefix extends the other; the longer prefix is the intersection. The cylinder of the empty prefix is all of C. Every cylinder splits into two smaller ones, C(s0) and C(s1). Finite unions of cylinders form the countable atomless Cantor algebra. Both operations here act on the selected cylinders.

There are 1 + 2 + ⋯ + 2ⁿ = 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1 prefixes through depth n. At n = 4, that gives 31 cylinder keys; one extra empty-set key makes 32 inputs. The empty prefix ∅ at the tree’s root (Q) selects C(∅) = C. The empty-set key (/) selects ∅ itself and has no tone. It absorbs intersections and is neutral in unions. No active keys leave the instrument idle and silent.

A ∩ ∅ = ∅   ·   A ∪ ∅ = A

A union is written as its unique maximal disjoint cylinder cover. Remove any cylinder already contained in another, then repeatedly merge sibling cylinders into their parent. This gives the same display and sound for equivalent sets, regardless of which keys produced them.

C(10) ∪ C(101) = C(10)
C(100) ∪ C(101) = C(10)
C(s0) ∪ C(s1) = C(s)

Cantor sound / Binary-tree harmonics

A prefix s of length n has binary value k, keeping leading zeros when counting its length. Number its node by 2ⁿ + k: the root is 1, its children are 2 and 3, and the next level is 4 through 7. Its sine-tone frequency is that harmonic of a 110 Hz fundamental:

f(s) = 110 (2ⁿ + k) Hz
f(∅) = 110 Hz   ·   f(0) = 220 Hz   ·   f(1) = 330 Hz
f(10) = 660 Hz   ·   f(101) = 1430 Hz

Each depth occupies the next octave band, 110 · 2ⁿ ≤ f < 110 · 2ⁿ⁺¹, with pitches increasing from left to right. Every child is higher than its parent, and distinct prefixes have distinct frequencies. This is a chosen sonification of the binary tree; the middle-third intervals do not physically vibrate at these frequencies.

The sound represents the simplified result, with one fixed-strength sine tone per cylinder. In Union, 100 and 001 sound together at 1320 and 990 Hz. Selecting 100 and 101 instead merges them into C(10), sounding only 660 Hz even if the 10 key is not held. Selecting 10 with 101 also gives C(10) in Union; in Intersection it gives C(101) at 1430 Hz. Incompatible prefixes in Intersection produce silence.

No active keys and an empty result are silent. The whole Cantor set sounds at 110 Hz, whether selected by Q or obtained by merging a complete cover. Holding or repeating a key does not increase a cylinder’s amplitude. Short attack and release envelopes smooth transitions. Songs, recorded playback, and video export use the currently selected operation; recordings retain key timings, so the same take can be explored in either mode.

The original library piece Branches Return is composed for Union. Its prefixes 000, 010, and 100 form a stable 880:1100:1320 Hz chord, the pure ratio 4:5:6. None contains another or forms a sibling pair, so all three tones remain. Other passages deliberately add siblings to merge voices downward. The closing gesture adds 01 to 00, then adds 1: the result moves from C(00) to C(0) to C, sounding 440, 220, then 110 Hz. Existing classical arrangements reuse their key timings in Cantor; the changed tuning and set operations do not preserve their original melodies or chords.

MIDI / Physical keys become addresses

Calibration assigns the keyboard’s leftmost MIDI note number m₀ to input 0. Each semitone step, including black keys, advances one input. Cantor uses indices 0 through 30 for prefixes in binary-tree order, followed by the empty set at index 31. The other views use indices 0 through 25.

i = m − m₀   ·   0 ≤ i < 32
For a Cantor prefix: fᵢ = 110 (i + 1) Hz   (0 ≤ i ≤ 30)

The MIDI note number is an input address, not a requested sound frequency. Frequencies still come from the selected model; Cantor still sounds only the simplified set. Positive note-on velocities all start with the same strength, and pitch bend is ignored. Holding advances Number exponents or keeps a modal driver on while its vibration builds toward equilibrium. Note-off, including note-on with velocity zero, releases the input; a sustain pedal (CC64) retains Number weights or keeps the modal driver on.

MIDI volume (CC7) controls the final master gain: a controller value v from 0 to 127 sets gain g = 0.5v/127, matching the Sound volume slider’s 0–50% range. Audio retains all 128 steps; the displayed percentage is rounded. Gain changes are smoothed with a 20 ms time constant. This scales overall loudness without changing pitches, driver weights, or the visualization. Volume messages on any channel of the selected device share this one master gain.

Note labels / A musical reference

For an audible frequency f, the nearest note is found on the twelve-tone equal-tempered grid with A4 = 440 Hz. Round its fractional MIDI position m to the nearest integer M; the signed difference c is measured in cents:

m = 69 + 12 log₂(f / 440)
M = round(m)   ·   c = 100 (m − M)

Labels show ≈ when |c| > 5. A scale match requires |c| ≤ 25 and a nearest-note pitch class belonging to the chosen scale. These are display thresholds. They never change a frequency, model, cylinder operation, or recording. The root is a pitch class, so matches repeat across octaves.

Scale intervals above the root, in semitones, are {0,2,4,5,7,9,11} for major, {0,2,3,5,7,8,10} for natural minor, {0,2,4,7,9} for major pentatonic, and {0,3,5,7,10} for minor pentatonic. Cantor’s 440, 550, and 660 Hz tones form the pure ratio 4:5:6. They match A major even though 550 Hz is about 13.7 cents below equal-tempered C♯5. A model’s available pitches need not supply every note of a scale.

04 / Square-plate modes

An eigenmode is a fixed spatial shape that can vibrate at a natural frequency. For an ideal thin square plate with simply supported edges, the basic shapes are sin(mπx) sin(nπy), and their frequency ratios follow m² + n². The labels (m,n) count half-waves in the two directions; + and − distinguish symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the swapped shapes.

ρh ü + D ∇⁴u = 0
D = Eh³ / [12(1 − ν²)]

Here E is Young’s modulus, ν is Poisson’s ratio, ρ is density, and h is thickness. The plate’s audible fundamental is chosen as 110 Hz. Colored nodes draws each selected mode’s zero lines separately; Mode mixture shows their signed sum; Vibration strength shows the square of that sum, including interference. These three displays do not simulate sand settling; the Sand particles display adds a separate transport model.

05 / Elastic-shell modes

A shell is a solid whose wall is thin compared with its overall size. Elastic means it returns toward its resting shape after a small deformation. Unlike a flat-plate model, a curved shell couples bending and stretching: moving one part outward can also pull on neighboring material.

This model is a freely suspended aluminum shell, 20 cm across, with a 3 cm outside corner radius and a 1 mm wall. A shell finite-element calculation includes bending, stretching, transverse shear, and mass, and solves for vector displacement patterns:

K uⱼ = λⱼ M uⱼ
λⱼ = ωⱼ²   ·   fⱼ = √λⱼ / (2π)

K describes stiffness and M describes mass. Each uⱼ is a mode shape; fⱼ is its natural frequency in hertz (cycles per second). The keyboard shows φⱼ and λⱼ, measured in s⁻²; for example, 3.28e6 means 3.28 × 10⁶ and corresponds to about 288 Hz. Symmetry allows different shapes to share an eigenvalue, so the mode index distinguishes them.

The six motions that translate or rotate the entire object without deforming it are excluded. The 26 retained elastic modes passed a flat-plate benchmark, rigid-motion and equation-residual checks, and mesh refinement; the frequency changes on refinement were below 1.16%.

The fixed cube displays each vector mode’s component perpendicular to the surface. Bright lines mark zeros of that normal component; material could still move tangentially there. Audio uses the calculated natural frequencies, but damping and the way sound travels into air are not calibrated to a real object.

Sand particles and Chladni figures

Grains on a vibrating plate move most in strongly excited regions and spend longer near quiet nodal lines. This view follows 6,000 individual grains with a lightweight stochastic transport model. It uses the selected plate’s mode shapes; it does not place grains on a precomputed outline or pull them toward a target curve.

J(x,y) = Σfrequency groups [Σj in group aⱼ φⱼ(x,y)]²
D(x,y) = D₀ J / (1 + J)
ΔX = √(2D Δt) ξ

Each key drives its labeled mode at the natural frequency shown on the keyboard. In the audio model, steady displacement amplitude is proportional to √wⱼ / rⱼ, where rⱼ is the frequency ratio. The resulting velocity amplitudes are proportional to √wⱼ. Here aⱼ is the normalized amplitude envelope of the same driven oscillator used for sound, including its buildup and ringdown. Equal-frequency modes share phase and are added before squaring; different frequencies contribute separate averaged powers. Thus a chord generally settles near shared quiet regions, not the zero set of an instantaneous signed sum. Slow beating between nearby frequencies is not resolved.

J is a normalized agitation measure, not calibrated mechanical energy. In normalized plate coordinates, D₀ = 0.014 sets a readable redistribution speed. Each horizontal step uses independent, zero-mean, unit-variance random increments ξ, evaluated at the grain’s current position, with a fixed time step of 1/120 second. This is an Itō diffusion approximation: where D is small, grains move less and accumulate statistically. With positive D and a reflecting boundary, its stationary density is proportional to 1/D; exact nodes are the limiting quiet locations. Once excitation fades to zero, grains remain where they are.

A reflecting rim keeps the grain count constant without changing the plate’s mechanical boundary conditions. Grain collisions, friction, actual flight heights, air currents, grain loading of the plate, and loss over an open edge are omitted. Positions are independent of sound volume, and timing is illustrative rather than a prediction for a particular sand or shaker. Recordings store the key performance; they do not store the grain positions or Redistribute sand gestures.

Free aluminum plate

Free plate models a 20 × 20 cm aluminum sheet, 1 mm thick, with all four edges free to move and rotate. The finite-element calculation uses Reissner–Mindlin plate bending with transverse shear and rotary inertia (MITC4 elements). No supports or edge springs are added. The thin-plate limit is D ∇⁴φ = ρh ω²φ; free-edge bending moments and effective transverse forces vanish.

K uⱼ = λⱼ M uⱼ
fⱼ = √λⱼ / (2π)

The keyboard labels φⱼ identify the computed bending modes; their pitches are the predicted frequencies in Hz, using E = 69 GPa, ν = 0.33, and ρ = 2700 kg/m³. Rigid translation perpendicular to the plate and two rigid tilts are excluded. In-plane modes are outside this bending model. Unlike the simply supported view, the free edges are not nodal lines.

The displayed shapes are transverse displacements, normalized for readability. Holding, Sustain, quick fades, and sound damping remain illustrative; this is not a calibrated recording. Sand particles uses these shapes in the transport approximation described above. The numerical solve is checked for rigid motions, equation residuals, mass orthogonality, mesh convergence, and agreement with analytical simply supported plate frequencies.

Sphere modes

Scalar surface uses real spherical harmonics Yℓᵐ on a sphere of normalized radius 1, satisfying −ΔₛYℓᵐ = ℓ(ℓ+1)Yℓᵐ. The keys show (ℓ,m): m = 0 is axisymmetric, positive m uses a cosine in longitude, and negative m a sine. The constant mode is omitted. Different patterns of the same degree share an eigenvalue; pitches use the illustrative mapping f = 110 λ/λ₁ Hz.

Elastic shell uses a freely suspended aluminum sphere, 20 cm outside diameter and 1 mm wall thickness, with the same material and MITC4 shell formulation as the rounded cube. The six rigid motions are excluded. The first 26 elastic modes have substantial radial motion: the displayed field is the normal component of their vector displacements, and sound uses their calculated natural frequencies. Purely tangential torsional modes would not appear in this display; they lie outside this selected set.

Spherical symmetry creates families of equal-frequency modes. The finite mesh slightly splits these frequencies and selects particular orientations; φ indices identify the numerical patterns. The 26-key limit includes only part of the last family. Validation compares two meshes and checks rigid motion, equation residuals, mass orthogonality, and the analytical lower-branch frequencies of spherical-shell bending theory. The geometry stays fixed, visual fades remain illustrative, and the calculation neglects air loading and acoustic radiation. Sound pitch optionally divides the elastic sphere’s audible frequencies by 4 or 8 (two or three octaves), without changing the calculated eigenvalues or shapes. Note labels follow the audible pitch; key details retain the natural frequency. The resonant buildup and decay use that same audible frequency in the visual envelope.

Scalar surface modes

Scalar surface solves the Laplace–Beltrami equation on the rounded cube: −Δₛφⱼ = λⱼφⱼ. Each mode is a single scalar field on the surface, rather than a vector displacement of a metal shell. Bright contours trace its zeros. These simpler patterns are useful for exploring geometry and superposition.

The eigenvalues use normalized geometry (width 2), not seconds⁻². Sound is an illustrative bending-style mapping fⱼ = 110 λⱼ/λ₁ Hz; it is not a prediction of this object's metal resonances. Both cube models use the same readable strength envelopes and fixed geometry.

06 / What grows and fades

Each physical-mode key switches on an oscillating force at that mode’s audible resonance. Its driver weight w starts at 1 and stays constant while held. Vibration builds because the driver supplies energy, then settles as damping balances that input. Release removes the force and leaves a decaying vibration. Sustain keeps the driver on; replaying a sustained key adds one unit of weight, up to 32. Songs and saved takes supply their recorded weights.

q̈ⱼ + 2γⱼ q̇ⱼ + ωⱼ² qⱼ = Fⱼ(t)
Fⱼ(t) = 2γⱼωⱼ (√wⱼ / rⱼ) cos(ωⱼt)
Aⱼ = rⱼ √[qⱼ² + (q̇ⱼ / ωⱼ)²]
display field = ∑ Aⱼ(t) φⱼ(x)

Here ωⱼ = 2πfⱼ and rⱼ = fⱼ/f₁. Sound and the display envelope solve the same damped, driven oscillator. At steady drive, displacement amplitude is √wⱼ/rⱼ and Aⱼ approaches √wⱼ. The × readout shows Aⱼ² while the key is active; it is a normalized strength, not a prime exponent or a measurement in physical units. A fresh held key therefore approaches ×1.0 instead of growing indefinitely. Releasing immediately unpresses the key while its sound and image ring down.

The damping γⱼ = 4 + 1.5√rⱼ s⁻¹ is an illustrative musical choice, not measured material damping. The object geometry stays fixed, and the display shows envelopes rather than fast oscillations. Single-mode nodal lines do not move when amplitude changes; surface contours are normalized for readability while their brightness follows strength. Frozen views show the selected steady patterns for inspection. Number retains its integer prime weights, and Cantor retains its fixed-strength set sonification.
